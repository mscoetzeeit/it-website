Word Processing Grade 10

Microsoft Word is the word processing application used in CAT. Grade 10 covers all the essentials — from creating documents and formatting text to working with tables, page layout and objects.

HOW TO READ THE STEPS ON THIS PAGE

When you see a path like Home tab→Font group→Bold it means: click the word Home at the top of the screen, then look in the section called Font, then click the Bold button. Keys you press on the keyboard look like this: Ctrl + S (hold the first key down, then tap the second).

Document Structure

A Word document is made up of:

  • Pages / Sections — the physical pages; sections allow different formatting in different parts
  • Paragraphs — blocks of text ending with a paragraph mark (¶)
  • Lines / Text — individual rows of characters
  • Objects — images, shapes, text boxes, charts inserted into the document
FORMATTING MARKS

Turn on formatting marks with Ctrl+Shift+8 or the ¶ button on the Home tab. This shows paragraph marks (¶), spaces (·) and tab characters (→) — very helpful for troubleshooting layout problems.

TYPOGRAPHY TIP

Use only ONE space after punctuation (full stops, commas, etc.) — NOT two spaces. Word's autocorrect will also fix "smart quotes" (curly ") and proper em-dashes (—) automatically.

Step-by-step: Make a heading bold, size 16, dark blue
  1. Move your mouse to the very start of the text you want to change. Press and hold the left mouse button, then drag across the words to highlight (the text turns a shaded colour). Let go of the button.
  2. At the top of the screen, click the Home tab (it is usually already open).
  3. Find the section labelled Font near the top-left. Click the small arrow next to the font size box (it shows a number like 11) and choose 16.
  4. Still in the Font group, click the B button to make the text bold. (Or press Ctrl + B.)
  5. Click the small arrow next to the A with the coloured line under it (the Font Colour button).
  6. A grid of colours appears. Click a dark blue square. If you cannot see the exact colour, click More Colours… and pick one.
  7. Click anywhere else on the page to deselect, and check your heading looks correct.

Spacing

  • Line spacing: controls space between lines within a paragraph (1.0 = single, 1.5, 2.0 = double)
  • Paragraph spacing: adds space Before or After a paragraph (set in Paragraph dialog: Layout → Paragraph)

Indents

  • Increase indent: Tab / Ctrl+M — moves paragraph to the right
  • Decrease indent: Shift+Tab / Ctrl+Shift+M — moves paragraph back left
  • First line indent: only the first line of the paragraph is indented
  • Hanging indent: first line is at the margin; all other lines are indented

Bullets and Numbering

  • Bullets: unordered list with symbols (•, ◆, ✓) — use when order doesn't matter
  • Numbering: ordered list (1, 2, 3 or a, b, c) — use when sequence matters

Step-by-step: Put your name in the header and a page number in the footer
  1. Click the Insert→Header button, then click Edit Header (or pick a built-in design). The cursor jumps into the header area at the top of the page.
  2. Type your name. It will now appear at the top of every page automatically.
  3. Click Go to Footer on the Header & Footer tab (or scroll down and click in the footer area).
  4. Click Page Number → Current Position → Plain Number. A page number appears.
  5. Click Close Header and Footer (the red X on the right) to go back to your normal document.

Quick Styles (Home Tab → Styles Group)

Styles apply a combination of formatting in one click. Built-in styles include Heading 1, Heading 2, Normal, Title. Using heading styles consistently enables automatic Table of Contents generation.

Working with Tables

Step-by-step: Insert a table with 3 columns and 4 rows
  1. Click in your document where you want the table to appear (the blinking line, called the cursor, shows where it will go).
  2. Click the Insert→Table button. A small grid of squares drops down.
  3. Move your mouse over the grid. As you move, squares light up to show the size. Move until 3 across and 4 down are highlighted (it says "3x4 Table" at the top).
  4. Click once. Word inserts an empty table with 3 columns and 4 rows.
  5. Click inside any block (called a cell ) and type. Press the Tab key to jump to the next cell. (Pressing Tab in the last cell makes a new row.)

Two new tabs appear whenever your cursor is inside a table: Table Design (colours, borders, styles) and Table Layout (add/delete rows, merge cells, alignment).

TEXT WRAPPING

When inserting pictures or shapes, change the text wrapping to control how text flows around the object. Common options: In Line with Text (moves with text), Square or Tight (text wraps around the object), Behind Text / In Front of Text.

Templates

A template is a ready-made document with the layout, fonts and placeholder text already done for you — like a pre-printed form you just fill in, instead of designing a letterhead from scratch every time you write a letter.

Step-by-step: Start a document from a template
  1. Click File → New.
  2. Browse the available templates (Letter, Fax, Report, etc.) or search online templates using the search box.
  3. Click a template to preview it, then click Create.
  4. Replace the placeholder text (often in square brackets, e.g. [Company Name] ) with your own details.

Watermark and Page Colour

A watermark is faint text or an image (like "DRAFT" or "CONFIDENTIAL") shown behind the normal text on every page — visible enough to notice, faint enough not to interfere with reading.

TaskHow

Remove a watermarkDesign → Watermark → Remove Watermark

Hyphenation

Hyphenation breaks long words across the end of a line with a hyphen, instead of pushing the whole word onto the next line — this keeps the right-hand edge of justified text looking neater.

  • Turn on automatic hyphenation: Layout → Hyphenation → Automatic
  • Turn off hyphenation: Layout → Hyphenation → None

Protecting a Document

Sometimes you want to share a document but stop people from changing it by accident — like handing someone a form where only certain blanks can be filled in.

Step-by-step: Restrict editing
  1. Click Review → Restrict Editing.
  2. Under "Editing restrictions", tick the checkbox and choose a restriction level (e.g. "No changes (Read only)").
  3. Click Yes, Start Enforcing Protection, then optionally type a password so only you can remove the protection later.
PASSWORD-PROTECTING THE FILE ITSELF

Restrict Editing protects the content while the file is open. To stop the file from opening at all without a password, use File → Info → Protect Document → Encrypt with Password instead.

EXAM TIP — Common Word Processing Tasks

In a practical exam, marks are awarded for precise formatting. Pay attention to: exact font sizes and types, correct alignment, proper header/footer content, correct use of styles, and whether you save in the correct format (.docx). Always save frequently!

Cell References

A cell reference identifies a specific cell by its column letter and row number. Using cell references in formulas is critical — if the data changes, the formula automatically recalculates.

WHY USE CELL REFERENCES?

Instead of typing =50+30, use =B2+C2. If the values in B2 or C2 change, the answer updates automatically. Hard-coded numbers never update.

Range Names

A named range gives a meaningful name to a group of cells. Select the cells → Name Box → type name → Enter. Then use the name in formulas:

Example
=SUM(Marks)   ← instead of =SUM(B2:B20)

Basic Functions

Functions are built-in formulas. Syntax: =FUNCTIONNAME(argument1, argument2, …)

Step-by-step: Add up a column of numbers with SUM
  1. Click the empty cell where you want the answer to appear (for example, the cell just below your list of numbers).
  2. Type an equals sign =. Every formula MUST start with =, otherwise Excel treats it as plain text.
  3. Type the word SUM followed by an opening bracket: =SUM(
  4. Now select the numbers: click the first number cell, hold the mouse button and drag down to the last number, then let go. Excel fills in the range for you, e.g. =SUM(B2:B10
  5. Type a closing bracket ) so it reads =SUM(B2:B10)
  6. Press Enter. The cell now shows the total. (Click the cell again and look at the Formula Bar at the top — it still shows the formula, not the answer.)
Try it yourself

Type the numbers 10, 20 and 30 into cells A1, A2 and A3. In cell A4 type =SUM(A1:A3) and press Enter . You should see 60 to see. Now change A1 to 100 — watch A4 update to 150 automatically. That is the power of using cell references!

AutoFill

AutoFill saves you from typing repetitive or predictable data by hand — like a list of dates, months, or numbers counting up — by recognising the pattern from one or two cells and continuing it for you.

Step-by-step: Use AutoFill
  1. Type the starting value (e.g. Monday , or 1 and 2 in two cells to show the pattern).
  2. Select the cell(s), then hover over the small green square at the bottom-right corner of the selection — the cursor turns into a thin black +.
  3. Click and drag down or across over the cells you want to fill. Excel continues the pattern (days, months, or counting numbers).
  4. To copy a formula instead of a list, AutoFill the same way — Excel automatically adjusts relative cell references for each new row.
AUTOFILL OPTIONS BUTTON

After dragging, a small button appears near the filled cells — click it to choose "Copy Cells" (repeats the exact same value) instead of "Fill Series" if you didn't want the pattern continued.

HTML & Web Design Grade 10

HTML (HyperText Markup Language) is the language used to create web pages. In CAT, HTML is coded in a text editor like Notepad++ and viewed in a browser.

What is HTML?

HTML = HyperText Markup Language. It uses tags (instructions wrapped in angle brackets) to structure and format content on a web page. Browsers read the HTML and display it visually.

HTML EDITOR

In CAT, use Notepad++ to write HTML (it highlights your code in colour). Save the file with a .html extension. Open it in a browser (Chrome, Edge) to see the result. You do NOT need an internet connection to view local HTML files.

Step-by-step: Create your first HTML file in Notepad++
  1. Open Notepad++ (click the Start button, type "Notepad++" and click it). A blank white page appears.
  2. Type your HTML code into the page (use the examples below).
  3. Click File→Save As at the top.
  4. In the File name box, type a name ending in .html — for example mypage.html (the .html part is very important, it tells the computer this is a web page).
  5. Click Save.
  6. Find your saved file in its folder and double-click it. It opens in a web browser (Chrome/Edge) so you can see the result.
  7. To make changes: edit the code in Notepad++, save again (Ctrl + S ), then go back to the browser and press F5 to refresh.

Tag Syntax

Most HTML tags come in pairs: an opening tag and a closing tag. The closing tag has a forward slash / before the tag name.

Syntax
<tagname>Content goes here</tagname>

Some tags are self-closing — they have no content and no closing tag: <br> and <hr>

Basic HTML Page Structure

Basic HTML structure
<html>
  <head>
    <title>Page Title Here</title>
  </head>
  <body>
    <!-- All visible content goes here -->
    <h1>My First Web Page</h1>
    <p>This is a paragraph.</p>
  </body>
</html>

Attributes

Attributes add extra information to a tag. They go inside the opening tag as name="value" pairs.

Attribute syntax
<tagname attribute="value">content</tagname>

The <font> tag controls the appearance of text using attributes:

</font>

Complete Example — Grade 10 HTML Page

Full example
<html>
<head>
  <title>Ms Coetzee CAT</title>
</head>
<body bgcolor="lightyellow">

  <h1><font color="darkblue">Welcome to Grade 10 CAT</font></h1>
  <hr width="80%">

  <h2>About this subject</h2>
  <p>CAT teaches us to use <b>application software</b> effectively.</p>
  <p>We study <i>Word, Excel, Access</i> and <u>HTML</u>.</p>

  <h3>Practical Tools:</h3>
  <p><font face="Arial" size="3" color="green">Microsoft Word</font></p>
  <p><font face="Arial" size="3" color="green">Microsoft Excel</font></p>

</body>
</html>
EXAM TIP — Common HTML Mistakes

1. Forgetting to close tags (e.g. <b> without </b> ) — everything after becomes bold.
2. Wrong quotes — use straight quotes " not curly quotes ".
3. Spelling colour as "color" — HTML uses American spelling: color , not colour.
4. <hr> and <br> do NOT need closing tags.
5. The <title> only changes the browser tab text — not what you see on the page.

Presentations Grade 10 · PAT only

Microsoft PowerPoint presentations are used in CAT only as part of the PAT (Practical Assessment Task). Presentations are NOT assessed in P1 exam papers — only in the PAT.

PAT ONLY — NOT IN EXAM PAPER 1

Presentations are taught in Term 3 as part of PAT Phase 1. They are used to present your Information Management findings. Presentations are assessed through the PAT, NOT in the June or end-of-year P1 exam.

What is a Presentation?

A presentation is a slideshow made up of individual slides, each containing text, images and other objects. PowerPoint (.pptx) is the standard format used in CAT.

The PowerPoint Interface

Ribbon
Home, Insert, Design, Transitions, Animations, Slide Show, Review, View

Rules for Good Presentations

  • Keep it simple — use keywords, not full sentences
  • Consistent design — use the same theme/colour scheme throughout
  • Limit content — 6×6 rule: max 6 bullet points per slide, 6 words per point
  • Use visuals — images, charts and diagrams are more engaging than plain text
  • Readable fonts — minimum size 24pt for body, 36pt+ for headings
  • High contrast — dark text on light background or vice versa
  • Avoid reading directly from the slides — they support, not replace, your presentation

Concepts of Computing (T1) Grade 10

This topic covers the fundamentals: what a computer is, types of computing devices, ICTs, and the difference between data and information.

Term 1

What is a Computer?

A computer is an electronic device that accepts data (input), processes it according to a set of instructions (program), stores the results and produces output. The basic model is the Information Processing Cycle:

→

Term 1

Types of Computing Devices

Multi-purpose Devices

DeviceDescription / Example
Desktop computerNon-portable; separate monitor, tower and keyboard; most powerful for the price; includes all-in-one designs
LaptopPortable, battery-powered; built-in screen and keyboard; includes convertible/2-in-1 (can fold into tablet mode)
TabletTouch screen; more portable than laptop; used with stylus or on-screen keyboard
SmartphonePocket-sized; phone + computer; uses mobile OS (Android, iOS); always connected
ServerPowerful computer that provides services to other computers on a network

Dedicated Devices (single-purpose)

Dedicated devices have an embedded computer designed for one specific function:

  • ATM — dispenses cash, checks balances
  • Digital camera — captures photos/video
  • Printer — prints documents
  • Fitness tracker / Smartwatch — monitors health data
  • Factory robots — automated manufacturing
  • Smart appliances — fridges, microwaves with embedded chips
CONCEPT OF CONVERGENCE

Convergence is when multiple functions are combined into one device. A smartphone is the best example — it is a phone, camera, GPS navigator, music player, internet browser and more, all in one device.

Term 1

What is ICT?

ICT (Information and Communication Technology) refers to technologies used to handle, store, retrieve, send and receive information. An ICT system has three core functions:

Convey
Transfer/communicate data between devices or people (e.g. sending an email, making a call)
Manipulate
Process and change data (e.g. calculating totals in a spreadsheet)
Store
Save data for later retrieval (e.g. saving a file to a hard drive or cloud)

ICTs in Everyday Life

  • Point of Sale (POS) system: barcode scanner + computer + card machine at a shop checkout
  • Cell phone system: phone + cell towers + network infrastructure
  • Online banking: computer/phone + internet + bank servers
  • School management system: stores learner records, attendance, marks
Term 1

Data vs Information

KEY DISTINCTION

Data = raw, unprocessed facts with no context (e.g. the numbers 85, 72, 90).
Information = data that has been processed and given meaning (e.g. "Learner achieved an average of 82% in CAT").
Remember: Data → processed → Information → used to make decisions (Knowledge).

Term 1

The GUI (Graphical User Interface)

A GUI is a visual interface that allows users to interact with the computer using icons, windows, menus and a mouse — instead of typing text commands. All modern operating systems (Windows, macOS, Android, iOS) use a GUI.

Desktop GUI features (Windows):

  • Desktop — the working area (like a physical desk)
  • Icons — small pictures representing programs or files
  • Taskbar — bar at the bottom showing open programs and the Start button
  • Start button — opens the menu to access all programs
  • Recycle Bin — holds deleted files until permanently removed
  • Shortcuts — quick access icons placed on the desktop
  • File Manager (This PC / Windows Explorer) — browse folders and files
EXAM TIP

Know the difference between data and information — this is a very common exam question. Always remember: data is raw, information is meaningful. Also be able to give examples from a school context (e.g. test marks = data; "learner passed Term 1" = information).

Common Exam Mistakes

Data and information are the same thing.
Data is raw, unprocessed facts; information is processed data that has meaning and helps you make a decision.
Hardware and software mean the same thing.
Hardware is the physical parts you can touch; software is the programs (instructions) that run on the hardware.
A tablet or smartphone is not really a computer.
It is a computing device — it takes input, processes data, gives output and has storage, just like a desktop.

Hardware (T1–T2) Grade 10

Hardware is every physical component of a computer that you can touch. It includes input devices, output devices, storage devices and the processing components inside the system unit.

Term 1

What is Hardware?

Hardware refers to the physical parts of a computer system. It works together with software (programs/instructions) to perform tasks. Hardware without software is useless, and software cannot run without hardware.

KEY DEFINITION

Hardware (T1–T2) = any physical component of a computer that you can touch and see. Peripheral devices = hardware devices connected to (but not part of) the main system unit, e.g. keyboard, mouse, printer.

COMPARING STORAGE MEDIA

When comparing storage, consider: Capacity (how much can it hold?), Speed (how fast does it read/write?), Robustness (how well does it withstand damage?), Volatility (does it lose data when power is cut?), Physical size (is it portable?), Cost.

EXAM TIP — Peripheral Devices

A peripheral device is any hardware device that connects to and is controlled by the computer but is not part of the core system unit. Examples: keyboard, mouse, printer, scanner, webcam. Remember: monitors ARE considered peripheral devices too.

Common Exam Mistakes

RAM stores files permanently.
RAM is temporary (volatile) — its contents are lost when the power is off. Storage (HDD/SSD) keeps files.
A scanner is an output device.
A scanner is an input device — it captures an image or text into the computer.
A bigger hard drive makes the computer faster.
More storage = more space, not more speed. More RAM or an SSD improves speed.

Software & Licensing (T1–T2) Grade 10

Software is the set of programs and instructions that tell hardware what to do. This topic covers the types of software, licensing agreements, and how operating systems work.

Term 1

What is Software?

Software = programs and instructions that tell the hardware how to operate. Without software, hardware is just an expensive collection of metal and plastic. Software is non-physical — you cannot touch it.

Term 1

Software Piracy and Copyright

ILLEGAL

Software piracy = copying, distributing or using software without a valid licence. This is illegal and unethical. It violates copyright law. Consequences include fines and criminal prosecution.

  • Copyright = legal protection given to creators over their original work. Software is automatically copyrighted.
  • Intellectual property = creations of the mind (music, art, software, inventions) protected by law.
  • Buying a software licence does NOT give you ownership — it gives you a right to use it under the specified conditions.
Term 2

System Software in Detail

Operating System (OS)

The operating system manages all hardware and software on the computer. It provides the user interface (GUI or command line) and coordinates all tasks.

Device TypeCommon Operating Systems
Desktop / LaptopWindows 10/11, macOS, Linux (Ubuntu)
Smartphone / TabletAndroid (Google), iOS (Apple)

Drivers

A driver is a small program that allows the operating system to communicate with a hardware device. When you plug in a new printer or mouse, the OS installs a driver for it. Without the correct driver, the device will not work.

Auto Configuration (Plug-and-Play)

Plug-and-Play (PnP) means the computer automatically detects and configures a new device when you connect it — without needing to manually install drivers or restart.

Utility Programs

Utility programs help maintain and optimise the computer:

  • Antivirus — detects and removes malware
  • Disk Cleanup — removes temporary files to free up space
  • Defragmenter — reorganises HDD files for faster access
  • File compression — reduces file sizes (e.g. WinZip, 7-Zip)
  • Backup software — creates copies of important data
  • Firewall — monitors network traffic to block threats

Networks (T2–T3) Grade 10

A network connects computers and devices so they can communicate and share resources. This topic covers types of networks, devices, communication media and how to obtain Internet access.

Term 2

What is a Network?

A computer network is two or more computing devices connected together to share resources and communicate. Networks can be wired (using cables) or wireless.

Aims and Objectives of Networks

  • Communication — send emails, messages, video calls
  • Share hardware — multiple computers share one printer or scanner
  • Share software — run programs stored on a central server
  • Share data/information — access files stored on a shared drive
  • Security — centralised access control; who can log in and what they can see
Term 2

Types of Networks

TypeFull NameCoverage AreaExample
PANPersonal Area NetworkA few metres (around a person)Phone connected to wireless headphones via Bluetooth
HANHome Area NetworkA homeDevices in your house connected to your home Wi-Fi router
LANLocal Area NetworkA building or campusSchool computer lab; office building
WANWide Area NetworkCountries / globalThe Internet is the largest WAN
PAN vs HAN

PAN: personal devices around ONE person (e.g. phone + smartwatch + earbuds). Range is typically under 10 m.
HAN: all devices in a home (phones, laptops, smart TV, smart fridge). Usually uses a home Wi-Fi router. Larger than a PAN.

 AdvantagesDisadvantages
PAN
  • Easy and quick to set up (e.g. Bluetooth pairing)
  • No cables needed — wireless and portable
  • Low cost — no special network equipment needed
  • Personal devices stay synchronised (e.g. phone and smartwatch)
  • Limited range — usually only a few metres
  • Data transfer speed can be slow (especially via Bluetooth)
  • Peripherals can usually only be paired with one device at a time
  • Not suitable for sharing resources among multiple users
HAN
  • Users can share data from any device in the home
  • Devices such as a printer can be shared by everyone — saves cost
  • An internet connection can be shared by all devices in the home
  • Easy to transfer files between devices
  • Security risk — if one device gets a virus it can spread to others
  • Dependent on the router — if it fails everyone loses connectivity
  • Setup complexity — can be difficult for inexperienced users
  • Privacy — other users in the home may see shared files
Term 2

Router
Directs data packets between different networks; connects your home/school network to the Internet; often combined with modem
Switch

Term 2

Communication Media (How devices connect)

Wired connections

  • Fibre optic — uses light pulses through glass/plastic; very fast; expensive; used for long distances and ISP backbones

Wireless connections

Wireless connection using radio waves; used in homes, schools, public hotspots; range typically 30–100 m indoors
Bluetooth
Short-range wireless (up to ~10 m); used to pair devices (phone to earbuds, keyboard, speaker)
NFC
Near Field Communication; very short range (~4 cm); used for tap-to-pay, access cards

EXAM TIP — apply it to the scenario

Recommend wired for a fixed PC that needs speed/stability (e.g. a school server or gaming PC), and wireless for devices that move around (e.g. a laptop, phone or tablet). Always justify your choice with a reason from the question.

Term 3

Pairing Devices

Pairing is the process of connecting two Bluetooth devices so they can communicate. Steps: enable Bluetooth on both devices → make one device discoverable → select it on the other device → confirm the pairing code.

Term 3

Obtaining Internet Access

To connect to the Internet you need:

  • A computing device (phone, laptop, tablet)
  • A modem/router OR mobile data (4G/5G)
  • An account with an ISP (Internet Service Provider)
ISP — Internet Service Provider

An ISP is a company that provides Internet access to homes and businesses. Examples in SA: Telkom, Vodacom, MTN, Afrihost, Rain. They provide a connection type (ADSL, fibre, LTE, 5G) and you pay a monthly fee for data or uncapped access.

Term 3

Basic Network Security

  • Password protection — only authorised users can log in
  • Firewall — software/hardware that monitors and filters network traffic
  • Encryption — scrambles data so it cannot be read if intercepted
  • Access rights — users can only access files/folders they are permitted to see
EXAM TIP — Advantages vs Disadvantages of Networks

Advantages:

  • Resource sharing — devices such as printers and storage drives can be shared by all users on the network, saving costs.
  • Communication — users can easily communicate with each other via email, instant messaging and video conferencing over the network.
  • Centralised data storage — all data is stored on a central server, making backup, security and access easier for all users.
  • Easy software updates — software can be updated on the server and it automatically applies to all devices on the network.

Disadvantages:

  • Expensive to set up — purchasing network infrastructure such as cables, switches and servers can be very costly.
  • Security risks — hackers can gain unauthorised access to the network, and viruses can spread rapidly from one device to all other devices on the network.
  • Dependent on the server — if the central server fails, all users on the network are unable to work or access their data.
  • Requires technical support — networks need specialised staff to set up, maintain and troubleshoot them.

EXAM TIP — when to use Bcc

Use Bcc when emailing many people who don't know each other (e.g. a newsletter), to protect their privacy and hide their email addresses from one another.

Email Distribution List (E-poslys)

An email distribution list is a list of people's email addresses grouped under a single group name. An email sent to the group name is automatically sent to all addresses on the list. This differs from a contact list — a contact list stores individual contacts, while a distribution list is specifically used to send messages to a group simultaneously.

EXAM TIP — Contact list vs Distribution list

Contact list: stores individual email addresses and contact details.
Distribution list (e-poslys): a group name you email, and it automatically goes to all members of the group.

Term 3

Netiquette (Internet Etiquette)

Netiquette = good manners and correct behaviour when communicating online. Rules include:

  • Do NOT type in ALL CAPS — it looks like you are SHOUTING
  • Check your spelling and grammar before sending
  • Be concise and courteous — do not write unnecessarily long messages
  • Do not gossip
  • Compress attachments before sending to reduce the file size
  • Use a clear and relevant subject line
  • Do not forward chain emails or spam
  • Respect others' privacy — do not share their email addresses without permission
Term 3

Safe Internet Use

  • Multi-level authentication: use more than one method to verify identity (password + OTP)
  • OTP (One-Time Password): a unique code sent to your phone for a single login attempt
  • Never share your passwords with anyone
  • Be suspicious of unexpected emails with links or attachments (phishing)
  • Only visit secure websites (HTTPS, padlock icon in browser)

Grade 10 · Theory (P2)

Social Implications (T1, T3–T4) Grade 10

Social implications looks at how ICTs affect people's health, the environment, society and legal/ethical choices. This topic integrates with all other theory areas.

Term 1

Ergonomics and Health

Ergonomics is the science of designing workspaces and equipment to fit the user safely and comfortably, reducing the risk of injury.

Correct Workstation Setup

  • Chair height: feet flat on the floor, knees at 90°
  • Monitor: at eye level, arm's length away, no glare
  • Keyboard: elbows at 90°, wrists straight (not bent)
  • Sit up straight — back supported by the chair
  • Take regular breaks — every 30–60 minutes, look away and stretch

Term 1

Green Computing

Green computing refers to using computers in an environmentally responsible way — reducing energy use and electronic waste (e-waste).

Green Computing Practices

  • Switch off monitors and computers when not in use
  • Use power-saving / sleep mode settings
  • Buy energy-efficient devices (Energy Star rating)
  • Print only when necessary (save paper)
  • Recycle old devices responsibly — do NOT throw in general waste
  • Donate still-working equipment to schools or charities
E-WASTE

E-waste (electronic waste) contains toxic substances like lead, mercury and cadmium. Improper disposal can contaminate soil and water. Always take old devices to registered recycling points.

Term 1

Economic Reasons for Using Computers

Computers save money and improve productivity in many ways:

  • Save paper — digital documents replace printed ones
  • Save labour costs — automated tasks replace manual work
  • Save communication costs — email and VoIP are cheaper than phone calls and postage
  • Efficiency — tasks are completed faster and with fewer errors
  • Accuracy — calculations and data entry are more reliable
  • Reliability — consistent output 24/7 without fatigue
Term 3

Digital Citizenship

Digital citizenship = being a responsible, ethical and safe user of technology and the Internet. It involves:

  • Behaving online as you would in person — respectfully and honestly
  • Protecting your own and others' personal information
  • Using technology for legitimate, legal purposes only
  • Respecting copyright and intellectual property
  • Reporting cyberbullying and online threats
Term 3

Digital Footprint

DEFINITION

A digital footprint is the trail of data you leave behind whenever you use the Internet — searches, social media posts, online purchases, websites visited. This data can be tracked, stored and used by companies and others. It is almost impossible to completely erase your digital footprint.

  • Active footprint: information you deliberately share (posts, profiles, sign-ups)
  • Passive footprint: information collected without your direct knowledge (cookies, browsing history, location data)
Term 3

The POPIA Act (Protection of Personal Information Act)

POPIA is a South African law that protects citizens' personal information. Key points:

  • Organisations must have a lawful reason to collect your personal data
  • Data must be stored securely and not shared without permission
  • You have the right to know what data is held about you
  • You can request that incorrect data be corrected or deleted

Personal information includes: name, ID number, email address, phone number, location data, health information, financial details.

Term 3

Safe Internet Use

Malware — Malicious Software

Virus

A program that attaches itself to other files and spreads when the file is executed. It can damage files, delete data or slow down the system.

How it spreads: Via infected email attachments, downloaded files, shared USB drives or infected websites.

Prevention: Use antivirus software and keep it updated; do not open unknown attachments; scan USB drives before use.

Worm
Similar to a virus but spreads automatically across networks without the user needing to open a file. Worms consume network and system resources and can bring a network to a halt. Prevention: firewall and updated software.
Trojan
A program that appears to be useful but actually contains malicious code. It opens a back door for hackers to access your system. It does not spread on its own — the user unknowingly installs it. Prevention: download software only from trusted sources; use antivirus software.

Online Threats and Fraud

ThreatDescriptionHow to Protect Yourself
Spam Unwanted mass emails usually sent for advertising or fraud. Can contain malicious attachments or links. Use a spam filter; do not click links in unknown emails; unsubscribe from unwanted lists.
Hoax / Scam A false message (email, social media) that tricks people into sending money, sharing personal information or spreading a virus. Often spread through chain letters or false prize notifications. Verify information before forwarding; be sceptical of offers that sound too good to be true; never send money to unknown parties.
Phishing Fraudulent emails or websites pretending to be legitimate organisations (e.g. your bank) to steal passwords, banking details or personal information. Check the sender's email address carefully; go to the website directly by typing the address, not by clicking a link; banks never ask for passwords via email.
Pharming An attack that manipulates DNS records so that even a correct web address redirects you to a fake website without your knowledge. Harder to detect than phishing. Use trusted DNS services; look for HTTPS and a valid security certificate; keep your router and operating system updated.
Email Spoofing The sender's email address is forged so the email appears to come from a trusted source (e.g. your employer or a bank). Often used in combination with phishing attacks. Look at the actual email address, not just the display name; be suspicious of urgent requests to take action; confirm via a separate channel if unsure.

Guidelines for Safe Internet Use

  • Use strong, unique passwords for each account and change them regularly
  • Enable two-factor authentication (2FA) where possible
  • Keep your operating system, browser and antivirus software updated
  • Use only secure (HTTPS) websites for any personal or financial transactions
  • Avoid public Wi-Fi for sensitive activities (e.g. internet banking)
  • Make regular backups of important data
  • Never click on links or attachments in unknown or suspicious emails
  • Download software only from legitimate, trusted websites
  • Report suspicious online activity to the relevant platform or authorities

Good Habits for Online Communication (Netiquette)

  • Be polite and respectful — treat others online as you would like to be treated
  • Use appropriate language — avoid offensive language in all online communication
  • Think before you post — posts are hard to remove and can damage your reputation
  • Respect privacy — do not share photos or information about others without their permission
  • Avoid cyberbullying — do not harass, exclude or intimidate others online; report it if you witness it
  • Check facts — do not forward unverified news or hoaxes; verify information before sharing
  • Use appropriate tone in email — use a subject line, greet the recipient and close properly
  • Limit personal information shared online — do not share your address, ID number or financial details on social media
REMEMBER: Stay Safe Online

No legitimate bank, government body or company will ever ask for your password, PIN or full banking details via email or phone. If someone does, it is a scam.
